1. Data & Atmospheric Physics

How Tempest-ARKNET ingests multi-source atmospheric records onto a 44-level AGL height ladder and derives hydrostatically exact density and sound speed.

1.1

Multi-source weather inputs

Tempest-ARKNET separates what it learns from (decades of gridded reanalysis and real weather balloons) from what steers each forecast (near-term NWP models and slow global climate drivers). Combining these sources lets one pipeline serve both launch-day operations and multi-month mission planning.

  • ERA5 reanalysis (historical backbone): 40+ years of hourly atmospheric profiles (1980–2026) merged from 13 Earthmover pressure levels plus 8 CDS boundary-layer levels (975–750 hPa) to resolve low-level inversions and wind shear.
  • IGRAv2 weather balloons (physical ground truth): Twice-daily (00Z / 12Z) real radiosonde soundings used for station bias correction and held-out verification, preventing the model from inheriting gridded reanalysis smoothness.
  • Operational NWP forecasts (near-term steering): Short- and medium-range guidance from HRRR (3 km convective scale, 0–48 h), GFS and GEFS (0–16 days), and ECMWF IFS when launch is within two weeks.
  • Global climate drivers (seasonal steering): Slow indices — ONI (El Niño / La Niña), QBO at 30 & 50 hPa (stratospheric wind phase), and WWV (equatorial Pacific warm water volume) — shift seasonal wind and jet-stream probabilities months ahead.

Design Principle

Cyclical sine/cosine pairs prevent artificial discontinuities at midnight or December 31, while climate drivers are always sampled jointly from historical records when future indices are unknown so physical teleconnections stay intact.

1.2

The 44-level vertical height ladder

Every generated weather realization lives on a fixed 44-level height-above-ground (AGL) ladder from 10 m to 18 km. Rather than spacing levels uniformly, Tempest-ARKNET concentrates 12 levels in the lowest 2 km where rockets are slowest and most sensitive to crosswinds.

44-Level AGL Altitude Ladder & Ascent Regimes

Why non-uniform AGL spacing matters: Rocket aerodynamic stability is most vulnerable during the first 2 km of flight. Packing 12 of the 44 levels below 2 km resolves pad-level wind shear without wasting model capacity on redundant upper-air levels.

  • Boundary-layer zone (12 dense levels, 10 m – 2 km): 10, 50, 100, 150, 200, 300, 500, 750, 1000, 1250, 1500, 2000 m. Tight steps near the pad capture rail-exit gusts, nocturnal low-level jets, and weathercocking torque.
  • Upper-air zone (32 uniform levels, 2.5 km – 18 km): Fixed 500 m spacing through the mid-troposphere, jet-stream core (9–13 km), and lower stratosphere for high-altitude apogee and drogue-chute drift.
  • Compact state vector ($D = 179$): Four vertical profiles — zonal wind u, meridional wind v, temperature T, and moisture RH (or lnd) across 44 levels ($4 \times 44 = 176$) — plus three surface anchors (t2m, sp, rh2m).
  • Derived analytically (not learned): Pressure $p(z)$, air density $\rho(z)$, speed of sound $a(z)$, specific humidity $q(z)$, and virtual temperature $T_v(z)$ are computed exactly from the 179-float state via hydrostatic thermodynamics.
1.3

Thermodynamics & dewpoint encoding

Tempest-ARKNET never asks the neural network to predict pressure, air density, or speed of sound directly. Instead, the generator predicts temperature, moisture, and surface pressure, and an exact thermodynamic solver derives every hydrostatic quantity aloft.

  • Why log1p(dpd) replaces raw RH: In dry desert air (like Spaceport America), raw relative humidity slams into the hard 0% boundary, creating truncated distributions and unphysical wall pile-ups. Encoding moisture as the log dewpoint depression keeps dry-air variance smooth and unconstrained.
  • Virtual temperature ($T_v$): Humid air is lighter than dry air because water vapor ($M_w \approx 18$) has lower molar mass than dry air ($M_d \approx 29$). Using $T_v$ accounts for moisture buoyancy cleanly in both pressure and density.
  • Two-pass hypsometric integration: Computing humidity $q$ requires pressure $p(z)$, while hydrostatic pressure $p(z)$ requires $T_v(q)$. Pass 1 integrates $p$ assuming dry air ($T_v = T$); Pass 2 recomputes $q$ and $T_v$ from that pressure and re-integrates, converging to under 0.01% pressure error.
Eq. 1.1 — Log Dewpoint Depression Moisture Coordinate $$\mathrm{lnd} = \ln\!\left(1 + \max(T - T_d,\, 0)\right)$$

Maps saturated air ($T = T_d$, 100% RH) to $0$ and stretches very dry desert layers smoothly without hard clipping at $0\%$ RH.

Eq. 1.2 — Virtual Temperature & Two-Pass Hypsometric Column $$T_v = T\left(1 + \left(\frac{R_v}{R_d} - 1\right) q\right) \approx T(1 + 0.6078\,q)$$ $$\ln p_{k+1} = \ln p_k - \frac{g(\bar{z}_k)\,\Delta z_k}{R_d\,\bar{T}_{v,k}}, \qquad \rho_k = \frac{p_k}{R_d\,T_{v,k}}, \qquad a_k = \sqrt{\gamma R_d\,T_{v,k}}$$

Integrating upward from surface pressure $p_0 = \mathrm{sp}$ with altitude-adjusted gravity $g(\bar{z}_k) = g_0 (R_E / (R_E + \bar{z}_{\text{ASL}}))^2$ guarantees every generated profile is hydrostatically consistent by construction.
