Metrics & Scoring Guide

A plain-English reference to every probabilistic metric in Tempest-ARKNET — how each score is computed, what healthy vs. broken ensembles look like, and why every metric protects rocket launch safety.

4.1

Accuracy + spread: CRPS & Energy Score

A single mean-wind forecast can look accurate on average while hiding catastrophic dispersion error. Tempest-ARKNET uses proper scoring rules that reward an ensemble only when both its center and its uncertainty width match reality.

  • CRPS (Continuous Ranked Probability Score):
    • Intuition: Generalizes mean absolute error (MAE) to a full forecast distribution, measuring the squared area between the ensemble CDF and the observed truth step.
    • How it is scored: Averages member-to-truth absolute error minus half the unbiased pairwise ensemble spread, reported in physical units (m/s, K, or %).
    • Good vs. bad: Lower is better ($0$ = exact deterministic bullseye); high values flag mean wind bias, overconfident narrow tails, or needlessly flat spread.
    • Why it matters for rockets: Catches both systematic wind bias (shifting the landing zone off-range) and overconfident narrow ensembles (understating impact dispersion).
  • Energy Score (ES):
    • Intuition: The multivariate extension of CRPS that scores the entire 179-element vertical profile vector $(u, v, T, \text{RH})$ simultaneously in Euclidean space.
    • How it is scored: Evaluates average $L_2$ vector distance to truth minus unbiased pairwise member $L_2$ spread, normalized by $1/\sqrt{D}$ so scores stay comparable across grid sizes.
    • Good vs. bad: Lower is better ($0$ = exact vertical profile match); elevated scores reveal cross-altitude or cross-variable mismatches even when single levels look accurate.
    • Why it matters for rockets: An ensemble can score well on single-height CRPS while pairing wrong surface winds with wrong upper-level jets; Energy Score penalizes joint column mismatches.
Fair CRPS & Unbiased Energy Score — tempest/scoring/score.py & model.py $$\mathrm{CRPS}_{\text{fair}}(F, y) = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M} |x_i - y| - \frac{1}{2M(M-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M} |x_i - x_j|$$ $$\mathrm{ES}(F, \mathbf{y}) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{D}}\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M} \|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{y}\|_2 - \frac{1}{M(M-1)}\sum_{i < j} \|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j\|_2\right)$$

The first term pulls ensemble members toward truth $y$; the second term rewards honest spread using the unbiased $M(M-1)$ denominator so small ensembles are never penalized for finite sample size, and $1/\sqrt{D}$ normalizes the $D$-dimensional Euclidean distance across altitude grids.

Geometric Meaning of CRPS: Area Between Ensemble CDF and Truth Step

CRPS equals $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \big(F(x) - \mathbf{1}\{x \ge y\}\big)^2\,dx$: the exact squared shaded mismatch between the ensemble CDF (sky blue) and the observed truth step (green).

4.2

Structure & honesty: Variogram, PIT & SSR

Even with a low CRPS, an ensemble can fail launch safety if it scrambles vertical wind shear or lies about its own confidence. Three structural diagnostics catch those failure modes directly.

  • Variogram Score:
    • Intuition: Checks whether simulated wind shear and thermal lapse rates between altitude pairs match the vertical structure of real radiosonde balloons.
    • How it is scored: Sums weighted squared errors between observed vertical differences $|y_i - y_j|^{0.5}$ and the ensemble-mean differences $|x_{m,i} - x_{m,j}|^{0.5}$.
    • Good vs. bad: Lower is better ($0$ = exact vertical gradient match); elevated scores flag broken cross-altitude correlation from independent level calibration.
    • Why it matters for rockets: Directly audits wind shear and lapse-rate realism — the vertical gradients that drive max-Q bending loads and staging attitude excursions.
  • PIT Histogram (Probability Integral Transform):
    • Intuition: Records which percentile bin of the forecast ensemble captured observed reality across hundreds of historical soundings.
    • How it is scored: Computes each sounding's empirical rank $u_k \in [0, 1]$ inside the $M$-member ensemble and histograms those ranks across equal quantile bins.
    • Good vs. bad: A flat (uniform) histogram means honest probabilities; a U-shape flags overconfidence (tail surprises) and a dome flags underconfidence (bloated spread).
    • Why it matters for rockets: Ensures reported 95th- and 99th-percentile dispersion bounds reflect true launch-day risk rather than hidden tail excursions.
  • Spread-Skill Ratio (SSR):
    • Intuition: Measures whether the ensemble's stated uncertainty width matches the actual size of its mean forecast errors.
    • How it is scored: Divides ensemble spread (root-mean ensemble variance $\sqrt{\overline{\mathrm{Var}(x)}}$) by ensemble-mean $\mathrm{RMSE}(\bar{x}, y)$.
    • Good vs. bad: Ideal = 1.00; values below $1.0$ indicate under-dispersed (overconfident) winds, while values above $1.0$ indicate over-dispersed forecasts.
    • Why it matters for rockets: Keeps Monte Carlo dispersion cones calibrated so range safety neither under-protects the corridor nor triggers unnecessary launch scrubs.
Variogram Score (p = 0.5), Empirical PIT Rank & Spread-Skill Ratio — tempest/scoring/score.py $$\mathrm{VS}_{0.5}(F, \mathbf{y}) = \sum_{i < j} w_{ij}\left(|y_i - y_j|^{0.5} - \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M} |x_{m,i} - x_{m,j}|^{0.5}\right)^2$$ $$u_k = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M} \mathbf{1}\{x_{k,m} \le y_k\}, \qquad \mathrm{SSR} = \frac{\sqrt{\overline{\mathrm{Var}(x)}}}{\mathrm{RMSE}(\bar{x}, y)}$$

In the Variogram Score, $w_{ij}$ weights each altitude pair $(i, j)$ (emphasizing adjacent layers where wind shear matters most), while $p = 0.5$ takes the square root of vertical differences so extreme upper-level jet jumps do not drown out mid-altitude shear errors. The PIT rank $u_k$ gives the fraction of ensemble members at or below truth $y_k$ (uniform on $[0, 1]$ when calibrated), and $\mathrm{SSR}$ compresses that dispersion shape into a single ratio centered at $1.00$.

4.3

Decision & launch-gate metrics: Brier Skill & Coverage

On launch day, range officers do not act on continuous wind distributions directly — they evaluate binary Go/No-Go gates (such as pad gusts exceeding 9 m/s or recovery drift crossing a range boundary). Decision metrics verify that Tempest-ARKNET's threshold probabilities are actionable.

  • Brier Skill Score (BSS):
    • Intuition: Evaluates how accurately the ensemble predicts binary Go/No-Go threshold exceedances relative to a naive climatology base rate.
    • How it is scored: Computes Brier Score $\mathrm{BS}$ (mean squared error between predicted exceedance probability $p_k \in [0, 1]$ and outcome $o_k \in \{0, 1\}$) and normalizes it as $1 - \mathrm{BS}/\mathrm{BS}_{\text{clim}}$.
    • Good vs. bad: Higher is better ($1.0$ = perfect, $0.0$ = no better than historical base rate, negative = worse than climatology).
    • Why it matters for rockets: Proves launch-gate exceedance probabilities (such as pad gusts above 9 m/s) add actionable Go/No-Go skill beyond seasonal averages.
  • Coverage (90% & 99% Bands):
    • Intuition: Tracks how often observed radiosonde profiles actually land inside the ensemble's nominal central 90% or 99% uncertainty bands.
    • How it is scored: Computes the empirical fraction of observed soundings falling between the lower and upper ensemble quantiles ([q05, q95] or [q005, q995]).
    • Good vs. bad: Ideal equals nominal coverage (90% or 99%); under-coverage exposes range safety to unmodeled outliers, while over-coverage wastes flyable launch windows.
    • Why it matters for rockets: Directly verifies that Monte Carlo impact ellipses and structural load envelopes achieve their advertised safety hit rates.
Brier Score, Brier Skill Score & Empirical Coverage $$\mathrm{BS} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N} (p_k - o_k)^2, \qquad \mathrm{BSS} = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{BS}}{\mathrm{BS}_{\text{clim}}}, \qquad \mathrm{Cov}_{1-\alpha} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N} \mathbf{1}\!\left\{q_{\alpha/2} \le y_k \le q_{1-\alpha/2}\right\}$$

Positive BSS proves the forecast adds real Go/No-Go decision value beyond seasonal averages; nominal $\mathrm{Cov}_{0.90} \approx 0.90$ and $\mathrm{Cov}_{0.99} \approx 0.99$ confirm that Monte Carlo safety ellipses match real-world hit rates.
